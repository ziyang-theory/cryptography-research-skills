# Prose and coherence

Use when drafting or revising an explanation whose sentences are individually clear but whose argument is difficult to follow. Apply the relevant diagnostic to the authorized passage; a local correction does not require a section-wide rewrite.

## Give paragraphs a purpose

Identify what the paragraph should enable the reader to understand: a distinction, an obstacle, the role of a construction step, or the consequence of a lemma. Naming its topic, such as “consistency checks,” does not identify that purpose. Check whether the sentences develop the intended point and whether the next paragraph can build on it.

A paragraph may combine setup, reasoning, and a conclusion when they serve one intelligible purpose. Use an opening or closing sentence to make that purpose visible when needed; do not impose a fixed paragraph template or add a summary that merely repeats the text.

## Connect familiar information to the new claim

Before shortening a difficult sentence, inspect its subject, main verb, and connection to the preceding sentence. Orient the reader using an object or claim already introduced, then develop the new information. Keep the subject and its principal action close enough that intervening notation or qualifications do not obscure their relationship.

Place emphasis deliberately. A sentence ending often draws attention; check that it emphasizes the intended claim rather than incidental notation. Keep a qualification beside the claim it limits even when another ordering sounds smoother. Never reorder quantifiers, sampling dependencies, or logical conditions to fit an information-flow pattern.

Use active or passive voice according to what the paragraph follows. Passive voice can preserve continuity when a value or operation remains the topic. Sentence length alone does not diagnose difficulty: splitting a sentence can obscure a dependency or leave a pronoun without a clear referent.

## Present corresponding cases in parallel

When cases or protocols have corresponding properties, present those properties in a comparable order and use stable technical names. This helps the reader locate the actual difference. Preserve an asymmetric explanation when the mathematics is asymmetric; matching prose must not imply identical hypotheses, interfaces, or guarantees.

## Revise at the scale of the problem

For a substantial revision, make a reverse outline: summarize each paragraph's purpose in one sentence, then read those summaries for missing connections, repetition, and prerequisites introduced too late. Adjust the organization before polishing sentences that may move or disappear. For a local edit, inspect only the affected passage and its transitions.

When reader feedback is available, use the reader's recovered claim and point of confusion as diagnostic evidence. Distinguish a missing explanation from a disputed mathematical step or a stylistic preference. A reader's proposed rewrite is one possible repair; check what caused the misunderstanding before adopting it.

## Optional source notes

These are adapted writing heuristics, not evidence of measured improvements in cryptography papers. Sources need not be reopened for routine editing.

- Gopen and Swan, [“The Science of Scientific Writing”](https://lsa.umich.edu/content/dam/sweetland-assets/sweetland-documents/Graduates/DWG/The-Science-of-Scientific-Writing.pdf), November–December 1990, pp. 552–558: reader expectations, continuity, emphasis, and the limits of mechanical rules.
- Ramsey, [*Teach Technical Writing in Two Hours per Week*](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/pubs/two.pdf), October 25, 2006, §4.2, Exercises E–H; §3.4: paragraph purpose, parallel structure, and diagnostic comments.
- Mensh and Kording, [“Ten Simple Rules for Structuring Papers”](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619), September 28, 2017, Rules 3–4 and 9–10: organization and revision. Its experimental-paper structure is not a required structure for theorem papers.
